### 6. Step notes

**Step 1.** Understand the economics: 2^7 full factorial = 128 runs; a 2^(7-4) fractional = 16 runs. The saving is real but it is paid for in resolving power.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 2.** Understand confounding (aliasing): in a fractional design, some effects are mathematically indistinguishable from others.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 3.** Learn the generator and defining relation — the algebra that determines exactly which effects are aliased with which.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 4.** Learn the resolution scale precisely. RESOLUTION III: main effects aliased with two-factor interactions. RESOLUTION IV: main effects clear of two-factor interactions, but two-factor interactions aliased with each other. RESOLUTION V: main effects and two-factor interactions all clear.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 5.** Understand the selection rule: use Resolution III only for early screening of many factors; use IV or V when you intend to interpret interactions.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 6.** Select 5 or more candidate factors from your capstone for a screening experiment.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 7.** Choose your fraction based on your run budget, and write down the resulting resolution BEFORE running anything.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 8.** Generate the design matrix and write out the complete alias structure. You must be able to state what each estimated effect is confounded with.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 9.** Apply effect sparsity and hierarchy: usually few factors dominate, and low-order effects are more likely to be real than high-order ones.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 10.** Randomise and execute the screening experiment.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 11.** Analyse the effects and identify the vital few factors. Where an important effect is confounded, state the ambiguity explicitly rather than assuming the main effect is the real one.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 12.** Resolve critical ambiguity with a fold-over design — a second fraction that de-aliases the effects you care about.

____________________________________________________________________________________

**Step 13.** Take the screened vital few factors forward to a full factorial or RSM study in Lab 25.

____________________________________________________________________________________

### 7. Self-check
